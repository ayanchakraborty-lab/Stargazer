Chapter 1

Introduction

A time series is a sequence of observations taken sequentially in time. What makes it different from an ordinary sample is the one thing classical statistics usually assumes away: order matters, and neighbouring observations are typically dependent on one another.

1.1  What a time series looks like

Every time series analysis starts the same way: plot the data. A time plot — observation on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal — instantly reveals features no summary statistic will: trend (a long-term drift up or down), seasonality (a pattern that repeats at fixed intervals, like a calendar quarter), sudden level shifts, outliers, and changing variability. Skipping this step is the single most common beginner mistake in the whole subject.

Series are usually classified as discrete (observed at fixed intervals, e.g. monthly unemployment) or continuous (recorded constantly, e.g. an ECG trace, though in practice almost everything gets digitised into a discrete series before analysis). A discrete series can arise by sampling a continuous process at intervals, or by aggregating it — e.g. summing daily rainfall into monthly totals.

1.2  Deterministic vs. stochastic

If a series can be predicted exactly from its past, it is deterministic — genuinely rare outside of physics. Almost every real series is stochastic: the future is only partly determined by the past, so instead of a single predicted number we can only describe a probability distribution for the next value, conditioned on what has already happened. This is the conceptual leap the whole subject is built on: a time series model is a statement about a distribution, not a formula.

✎ Worked example

A reservoir's daily inflow depends on rainfall, snowmelt and upstream releases — no formula predicts it exactly, so it's modelled stochastically: today's inflow is drawn from a distribution whose mean and spread depend on recent history (was it a wet week? is it spring thaw?). Contrast this with the position of a pendulum: given its current position and velocity, physics predicts its position at any future time exactly — deterministic.

1.3  Objectives of time-series analysis

Four goals recur, and most projects are really pursuing one of them:

Description — summarise the main features (trend, seasonality, turning points) as a first pass, often before any formal model is fitted.
Explanation — when two or more series are observed together, use variation in one to explain variation in another (e.g. does advertising spend explain sales?).
Prediction / forecasting — arguably the most common use in practice: given the observed past, forecast future values, usually with some measure of uncertainty attached.
Control — when a series measures a process we can intervene in (e.g. a chemical plant's output), use the model to keep the process on target.

Exercises

1.1
A city's monthly electricity demand is recorded for 20 years. You're told "just fit a straight line through it and extrapolate." Explain, in terms of the four objectives above, why this instruction conflates two different goals — and why doing only one of them well can still produce a bad forecast.
🔒 Reveal answer
Fitting a straight line is description — a compact summary of the dominant trend. Forecasting is a separate objective with its own requirement: a good description of the past doesn't guarantee a good model of the mechanism generating the future. Electricity demand has strong seasonality (heating/cooling load) that a trend line ignores entirely; extrapolating the trend alone will systematically mis-forecast every summer and winter. A good forecast needs a model that captures trend and seasonality and the correlation structure of what's left over — description is only the first step, not a substitute for the rest.
1.2
Two series are sampled and aggregated versions of the same underlying continuous rainfall process: one is "rainfall intensity at midnight each day," the other is "total rainfall each day." Which one would you expect to look "smoother," and why does the answer matter for choosing a model?
🔒 Reveal answer
The aggregated (daily total) series is smoother. Sampling a single instant captures whatever noise happens to be present at that moment, while aggregating averages out high-frequency fluctuation over the interval — the same reason a moving average smooths a plot. This matters because aggregation changes the correlation structure: a process that looks like independent noise when sampled can look strongly autocorrelated once aggregated, purely as an artefact of the aggregation window overlapping between consecutive totals. Choosing a model without knowing which one you have risks fitting structure that is really a measurement artefact.
1.3
"If a process is stochastic, nothing about its future can be known." Give a precise, one-sentence correction to this statement using the language of Section 1.2.
🔒 Reveal answer
A stochastic process's future is not known exactly, but it is known as a conditional probability distribution given the past — so quantities like the mean, variance, and prediction intervals of the future value are entirely knowable, even though the single realised value is not.
Chapter 2

Simple Descriptive Techniques

Before any model touches the data, description does the heavy lifting: separate a series into the parts that are easy to explain (trend, seasonality) and the part that isn't (the residual) — and check whether that leftover part still has structure worth modelling.

2.1–2.2  Types of variation, and what "stationary" means (informally)

A series' variation is usually decomposed into: trend (a slow, long-term change in level), seasonal variation (a pattern that repeats with a fixed, known period), cyclic variation (rises and falls with no fixed period, like a business cycle), and everything left over, the residual or irregular component.

A series is (informally, for now) called stationary if it has no trend or seasonality — it fluctuates around a constant mean with roughly constant variability forever. This is a working, descriptive idea here; Chapter 3 gives it a precise probabilistic definition. Almost all time-series theory is built for stationary series, which is exactly why so much of the descriptive toolkit exists to strip away trend and seasonality until what's left behaves like one.

2.4  Transformations

When the amount of variability grows as the level of the series grows (a shop's monthly sales swing by more, in absolute terms, in December than in February — but proportionally similarly), a variance-stabilising transformation is applied before anything else, most commonly the logarithm — turning multiplicative seasonal effects into additive ones — or, more generally, a Box–Cox transformation.

\[ y_t^{(\lambda)} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{y_t^{\lambda}-1}{\lambda} & \lambda \ne 0 \\[4pt] \ln y_t & \lambda = 0 \end{cases} \]
Box–Cox transformation
2.5–2.6  Estimating trend and seasonal variation

The classical approach is a moving average: replace each observation with the (weighted) average of itself and its neighbours, which cancels out the seasonal and irregular components and leaves an estimate of trend. For monthly data a 12-point centred moving average is standard, since it exactly spans one full seasonal cycle.

\[ \hat{m}_t = \frac{1}{12}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}x_{t-6} + x_{t-5} + \cdots + x_{t+5} + \tfrac{1}{2}x_{t+6}\right) \]
Centred 12-point moving average (monthly trend estimate)

Once trend is removed (subtracted, if additive; divided out, if multiplicative), the seasonal index for each calendar month is estimated by averaging the detrended values for that month across all years — then, whatever remains is the irregular component.

2.7–2.8  Autocorrelation and tests of randomness

The single most important descriptive tool in the whole subject is the sample autocorrelation function (ACF, sometimes called the correlogram): the correlation of the series with itself, lagged by k time steps, for k = 1, 2, 3, ….

\[ r_k = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n-k}(x_t-\bar{x})(x_{t+k}-\bar{x})}{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(x_t-\bar{x})^2} \]
Sample autocorrelation coefficient at lag k

Plotted against k, this is the fingerprint used throughout the rest of the book to identify what kind of model a series needs — pure noise gives an ACF that's essentially zero everywhere except lag 0; trend gives an ACF that dies away very slowly; seasonality gives an ACF with spikes at the seasonal lag and its multiples. A formal check that a series is indistinguishable from white noise is the Ljung–Box test:

\[ Q = n(n+2)\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{r_k^2}{n-k} \ \sim\ \chi^2_{K} \text{ under } H_0 \]
Ljung–Box portmanteau statistic (degrees of freedom reduced by any fitted parameters)
✎ Worked example

A café records daily footfall for a year and computes the ACF. It shows a strong spike at lag 7 (and a weaker one at lag 14) but is otherwise flat — the classic signature of a weekly seasonal pattern (Saturdays are busy every single week) sitting on top of essentially random day-to-day noise. No spike at lag 1 tells you today's footfall, once you know the day of the week, tells you almost nothing extra about tomorrow's.

Exercises

2.1
You detrend a series by subtracting a 12-month moving average, then compute the ACF of the residual and find a strong negative spike at lag 1 that wasn't obviously there before. Is this evidence the residual is not random noise, or could the detrending procedure itself have introduced it? Explain the mechanism either way.
🔒 Reveal answer
It can easily be an artefact of the detrending, not a real feature of the underlying noise. A moving average is itself a smoothing filter that induces correlation between adjacent residuals: subtracting a smooth estimate of trend from a noisy series tends to make consecutive residuals swing in opposite directions (if the trend estimate slightly overshoots at one point it tends to undershoot at the next), producing a spurious negative lag-1 autocorrelation. This is exactly why "eyeball a spike in the ACF" isn't sufficient — you have to reason about what your own preprocessing could have manufactured before concluding the data itself has structure.
2.2
Two shops report monthly revenue. Shop A's seasonal swing is roughly ±$2,000 every December regardless of overall sales level; Shop B's swing is roughly ±15% of that month's trend level. Which transformation, if any, would you apply before decomposing each — and why would applying Shop B's transformation to Shop A's data be a mistake?
🔒 Reveal answer
Shop A has an additive seasonal effect (constant absolute size) — decompose directly, no transform needed. Shop B has a multiplicative effect (size scales with level) — take logs first, which converts a multiplicative seasonal effect into an additive one on the log scale, then decompose additively. Log-transforming Shop A's data would be a mistake because its seasonal swing genuinely doesn't scale with level; the log transform would make the December effect look like it shrinks as trend grows (since a fixed $2,000 is a shrinking percentage of a rising trend), inventing a change in seasonal structure that isn't really there.
2.3
Sketch (in words) what the sample ACF of a series consisting of pure linear trend plus a tiny amount of noise would look like out to lag 20, and explain why this shape makes the ACF a poor tool for spotting subtle autocorrelation structure until trend has been removed.
🔒 Reveal answer
The ACF would start near 1 at lag 1 and decay only very slowly and almost linearly out to lag 20, staying large and positive throughout — because a trending series is highly correlated with a lagged copy of itself at essentially every lag (both are dominated by the same upward drift). This shape swamps everything else: any genuine short-range dependence in the noise component is completely masked by the trend-induced correlation, which is why trend must be removed before the ACF is used diagnostically — otherwise the ACF just tells you "there's a trend," which you already knew from the time plot.
Chapter 3

Probability Models for Time Series

Chapter 2 described one observed series. This chapter builds the machinery to talk about the process that could have generated it — the entire population of series it might have been, of which we only ever see one draw.

3.1  Stochastic processes

A stochastic process is a family of random variables \(\{X_t\}\) indexed by time. The observed data \(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\) is treated as one realisation from this process — the same way a single coin flip is one realisation of a Bernoulli random variable. The catch that makes time series hard: with ordinary i.i.d. sampling you can draw as many independent realisations as you like; with time series you typically get exactly one realisation, of finite length, and have to infer the properties of the whole process from it. This is only possible if the process has enough regularity — which is precisely what stationarity provides.

3.2  Stationary processes (the precise version)

A process is strictly stationary if the joint distribution of any collection of observations is unchanged by shifting all their time indices by the same amount. This is usually too strong to check in practice, so we instead use second-order (weak) stationarity: constant mean, and an autocovariance that depends only on the lag \(k\), not on absolute time \(t\).

\[ E[X_t] = \mu \ \ \text{(constant)}, \qquad \gamma_k = \operatorname{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+k}) \ \ \text{(depends only on } k \text{)} \]
Conditions for second-order stationarity
3.3  The autocorrelation function

For a stationary process, the theoretical autocorrelation function is \(\rho_k = \gamma_k / \gamma_0\) — the population version of the sample \(r_k\) from Chapter 2. Every model below is really just a different way of specifying what \(\rho_k\) looks like.

3.4  Some useful stochastic processes

White noise — a sequence of uncorrelated random variables with constant mean (usually 0) and variance \(\sigma^2\): the building block every other model is constructed from. \(\rho_k = 0\) for all \(k \ne 0\).

Random walk — \(X_t = X_{t-1} + Z_t\), a running sum of white noise. It is not stationary (its variance grows with \(t\)), but its first difference, \(X_t - X_{t-1} = Z_t\), is just white noise — the single most important fact about non-stationary series in the entire book, since it justifies differencing as a cure.

Moving average, MA(q) — the current value is a finite linear combination of the current and past \(q\) noise terms:

\[ X_t = Z_t + \theta_1 Z_{t-1} + \cdots + \theta_q Z_{t-q} \]

MA(\(q\)) has a sharp signature: \(\rho_k = 0\) for every \(k > q\) — the ACF "cuts off" abruptly.

Autoregressive, AR(p) — the current value is a linear combination of its own past values plus fresh noise:

\[ X_t = \phi_1 X_{t-1} + \cdots + \phi_p X_{t-p} + Z_t \]

Unlike MA, AR's ACF never cuts off exactly to zero — it decays gradually (geometrically, or as a damped sine wave for higher orders). For AR(1) specifically, \(\rho_k = \phi^k\), and the process is stationary only if \(|\phi| < 1\).

ARMA(p,q) combines both: \(X_t = \phi_1 X_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi_p X_{t-p} + Z_t + \theta_1 Z_{t-1} + \cdots + \theta_q Z_{t-q}\) — often achieving a good fit with far fewer parameters than a pure AR or MA model would need. ARIMA(p,d,q) extends this to non-stationary series: difference \(d\) times until stationary, then fit ARMA(p,q) to the result.

3.5  The Wold decomposition theorem

A remarkable unifying result: any discrete stationary process (with no purely deterministic component) can be written as an infinite-order moving average of white noise, \(X_t = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j Z_{t-j}\). This is why MA, AR and ARMA models are not just three arbitrary choices among many — a stationary AR(p) process, for instance, is itself expressible as an MA(∞); fitting a finite AR or ARMA model is really just finding a parsimonious way to approximate this infinite representation.

Exercises

3.1
A colleague says "I fitted an AR(1) with φ = 1.2 because it gave the best in-sample fit." Explain precisely what goes wrong with this model, and why a good in-sample fit doesn't rescue it.
🔒 Reveal answer
|φ| < 1 is the stationarity condition for AR(1); with φ = 1.2 the process is explosive — its variance grows without bound as t increases, and it isn't a valid stationary model at all. It can still fit the observed in-sample stretch reasonably well, because over a short enough window an explosive process can resemble a well-behaved one, but it is a statement about instability, not about a genuine mean-reverting dependence structure, and it will produce forecasts and prediction intervals that blow up unrealistically out of sample.
3.2
You are shown a sample ACF that is essentially zero at every lag except a single significant spike at lag 1. A colleague immediately says "AR(1)." Explain why "MA(1)" is at least as good a guess from this evidence alone, and describe the one additional plot that would let you choose between them.
🔒 Reveal answer
An MA(1)'s ACF is exactly zero beyond lag 1 by construction — an ACF with one clean spike and nothing afterwards is precisely the MA(1) signature, not merely consistent with it. An AR(1) can also produce an ACF that looks like this if φ is small (it decays so fast that lags beyond 1 are negligible), so the ACF alone is genuinely ambiguous here. The PACF resolves it: AR(1)'s PACF cuts off sharply after lag 1, while MA(1)'s PACF decays gradually — so plotting the PACF is the missing diagnostic.
3.3
Using the Wold decomposition, explain why "just use a very high-order AR model for everything" is a defensible (if inefficient) strategy, while "just use a very high-order MA model for everything" is not equally defensible as a universal substitute.
🔒 Reveal answer
Wold's theorem guarantees every stationary process has an MA(∞) representation — so in principle a high-order MA model is the more directly justified universal approximation, not the AR one. In practice, though, a finite-order AR model is often a far more parsimonious approximation of that same MA(∞) representation, because inverting the AR polynomial produces an infinite MA expansion with rapidly decaying coefficients — so a low-order AR model implicitly represents a long MA memory with very few parameters, which is exactly why "throw a large AR order at it" tends to work reasonably well in practice, while forcing a pure high-order MA fit is typically far less efficient, requiring many more estimated parameters to capture the same structure.
Chapter 4

Estimation in the Time Domain

Chapter 3 gave us a menu of models. This chapter is about the practical business of taking one real, finite series and turning that menu into a single fitted model — the classical Box–Jenkins methodology.

The Box–Jenkins cycle: identify → estimate → check → (loop until residuals look like white noise) → forecast.
4.1–4.3  Identification: estimating the ACF and fitting AR / MA

Section 3.4's ACF/PACF signatures become, in practice, a lookup table: plot the sample \(r_k\) and partial autocorrelations, compare their shape to the table in Chapter 3, and read off candidate orders \(p\) and \(q\). For a pure AR(p), the parameters can be estimated by solving the Yule–Walker equations — a linear system relating the \(\phi_i\) to the theoretical \(\rho_k\), with the sample \(r_k\) substituted in:

\[ \rho_k = \phi_1\rho_{k-1} + \phi_2\rho_{k-2} + \cdots + \phi_p \rho_{k-p}, \quad k = 1,\ldots,p \]

MA parameters have no such closed-form shortcut — fitting an MA(q) already requires iterative numerical methods, because the MA likelihood is nonlinear in \(\theta\).

4.4–4.6  Fitting ARMA, ARIMA, and seasonal (SARIMA) models

Full ARMA(p,q) fitting is done by maximum likelihood (or close approximations to it), since neither Yule–Walker nor simple regression handles the moving-average part. ARIMA(p,d,q) simply differences the series \(d\) times first, then fits ARMA(p,q) to the result. When seasonality is also present, the model extends to SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)\(_s\): a second, seasonal AR/I/MA structure operating at lag \(s\) (e.g. \(s=12\) for monthly data) layered on top of the ordinary one — this single model class, refined in the 1970s, is still a default first choice in applied forecasting today.

4.7–4.8  Residual analysis and general remarks on model building

A fitted model is only trustworthy if its residuals are indistinguishable from white noise — checked by plotting the residual ACF and running a Ljung–Box test on it (Chapter 2). If the residuals still show structure, the model is misspecified and identification must be revisited.

Among competing models that pass this check, prefer the more parsimonious one (fewer parameters) — formalised by information criteria such as

\[ \text{AIC} = -2\ln(\hat{L}) + 2k \]

where \(\hat L\) is the maximised likelihood and \(k\) the number of fitted parameters: a penalty for complexity that stops you from "fitting the noise" with an unnecessarily large model.

Common pitfall — overfitting. Adding more AR or MA terms will (almost) always improve the in-sample fit. That is not evidence the extra terms are real; it's exactly why AIC penalises parameter count instead of judging on fit alone, and why residual whiteness, not R², is the acceptance criterion.

Exercises

4.1
A fitted ARIMA(2,1,1) model has residuals that pass the Ljung–Box test comfortably. Does this prove the model is "correct"? Give a concrete example of a way the model could still be wrong despite passing this check.
🔒 Reveal answer
No — passing Ljung–Box only shows the residuals are uncorrelated (consistent with white noise), which is necessary but not sufficient for correctness. It says nothing about, for example, whether the residual variance changes over time (conditional heteroscedasticity, addressed by ARCH/GARCH models in Chapter 11) — a residual series can be perfectly uncorrelated at every lag while still having volatility clustering, meaning large residuals bunch together in time even though they don't correlate linearly. The model would sail through Ljung–Box while still badly understating risk during volatile periods.
4.2
Two models fit the same series: Model A has a lower AIC but slightly worse in-sample sum of squared errors than Model B. Which one should you generally prefer, and what does this tell you about what AIC is actually trading off?
🔒 Reveal answer
Prefer Model A. AIC is explicitly trading off fit against complexity (via the \(2k\) penalty term) — a lower AIC despite a worse raw fit means Model A achieves nearly as good a fit with fewer parameters, which is exactly the parsimony principle in Section 4.8. Model B's better in-sample error likely reflects it fitting sample-specific noise rather than genuine structure, which typically costs it more than it gains once you look at out-of-sample forecasting performance.
4.3
Why can't the Yule–Walker equations be used, unmodified, to estimate the parameters of a pure MA(q) model?
🔒 Reveal answer
The Yule–Walker equations exploit a linear relationship between the ACF and the AR coefficients — the ACF at lag k is literally a linear combination of the AR parameters and lower-lag ACF values. An MA(q) process's ACF is a nonlinear function of the θ parameters (e.g. for MA(1), \(\rho_1 = \theta/(1+\theta^2)\) — θ appears in both numerator and a nonlinear denominator), so there is no equivalent linear system to invert; the θ's must instead be found by nonlinear optimisation of the likelihood.
Chapter 5

Forecasting

For most practitioners, forecasting is time series analysis — everything in Chapters 1–4 exists to produce a defensible number (and a defensible margin of error) for what happens next.

5.2  Univariate procedures

Simple exponential smoothing forecasts the next value as a weighted average of all past observations, with weights decaying geometrically into the past — recent data matters more, but nothing is ever fully discarded:

\[ \hat{x}_{t+1} = \alpha x_t + (1-\alpha)\hat{x}_t, \qquad 0 < \alpha < 1 \]

A high \(\alpha\) tracks recent changes closely but is noisy; a low \(\alpha\) is smooth but slow to react — this single dial is the entire trade-off. Plain exponential smoothing assumes no trend or seasonality; Holt's linear method adds a second smoothed component for trend, and Holt–Winters adds a third for seasonality, each with its own smoothing constant (\(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell_t &= \alpha x_t + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1}) \\ b_t &= \beta(\ell_t-\ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta)b_{t-1} \\ \hat{x}_{t+h} &= \ell_t + h\,b_t \end{aligned} \]
Holt's linear trend method

The alternative is Box–Jenkins forecasting: once an ARIMA model is fitted (Chapter 4), the minimum mean square error forecast is simply its conditional expectation given the observed past — exponential smoothing turns out to be a special case, exactly equivalent to the forecast from an ARIMA(0,1,1) model.

5.3–5.4  Multivariate procedures, and comparing methods

When related series are available (e.g. leading indicators), multivariate approaches — regression with time-series errors, transfer-function models (Chapter 9), or full vector models (Chapter 12) — can forecast a series using information beyond its own past. In practice, no single method dominates: large forecasting competitions repeatedly find that simple methods (exponential smoothing variants) are highly competitive against sophisticated ones, especially at short lead times and on noisy series — sophistication buys the most when the series is long, clean, and has real dynamic structure to exploit.

5.6  Prediction theory and prediction intervals

The minimum mean square error forecast of \(X_{t+h}\) given data up to time \(t\) is always the conditional expectation \(E[X_{t+h}\mid X_t, X_{t-1}, \ldots]\) — this is the theoretical justification for every specific forecasting formula above. A point forecast alone is incomplete without a measure of uncertainty; for a model with 1-step-ahead forecast error variance \(\sigma^2\), an approximate 95% prediction interval is

\[ \hat{x}_{t+h} \pm 1.96\,\sigma_h \]

where \(\sigma_h\) (the \(h\)-step forecast standard error) typically grows with the lead time \(h\) — which is exactly why credible forecasts widen into a cone the further out they look.

Common pitfall — reporting a point forecast with no interval implies a confidence that essentially never exists in practice. A forecast "$1.2M in Q3" is close to meaningless without also stating something like "$1.0M–$1.4M."

Exercises

5.1
A retailer uses simple exponential smoothing (no trend, no seasonal term) to forecast monthly sales of a product whose popularity is genuinely, steadily rising. Explain precisely why every one of their forecasts will be a systematic underestimate, using the recursive formula.
🔒 Reveal answer
Simple exponential smoothing's forecast for every future period is just the current smoothed level — a flat line — because the recursion has no mechanism for extrapolating a slope. If the true process has a genuine upward trend, the smoothed level \(\hat x_{t+1}\) is a weighted average of past values, which are systematically below the current true level whenever there's real upward drift, so the forecast lags the trend and is biased low at every horizon — this is precisely the gap Holt's method closes by adding an explicit trend term \(b_t\).
5.2
Why does a sensible prediction interval almost always widen as the forecast horizon h increases, even for a perfectly well-specified model?
🔒 Reveal answer
Each additional step ahead introduces one more, as-yet-unobserved noise term into the forecast — an h-step-ahead forecast error is a sum of h (weighted) future shocks, none of which are known at the time the forecast is made, so its variance accumulates with h. Even with the true model and true parameters, uncertainty about outcomes further in the future is fundamentally greater than uncertainty about the very next observation, because there is simply more unrealised randomness standing between "now" and "then."
5.3
A forecasting competition finds that on very short, noisy monthly series, simple exponential smoothing beats a carefully fitted SARIMA model out-of-sample, even though the SARIMA model had a lower AIC in-sample. Reconcile these two facts.
🔒 Reveal answer
AIC measures in-sample fit penalised for parameter count, evaluated on the same data used to estimate the model — it is not a direct measure of genuine out-of-sample forecasting accuracy, especially with a short series, where a more flexible model can fit sample-specific noise that a lower-AIC choice still partly captures. A short series simply doesn't contain enough information to reliably estimate the extra SARIMA parameters, so the estimation error in those parameters can outweigh the theoretical advantage of a "more correct" model structure — the simpler method's very inflexibility becomes a virtue when data is scarce.
Chapter 6

Stationary Processes in the Frequency Domain

Everything so far has described a series in the time domain: how does \(X_t\) relate to \(X_{t-1}, X_{t-2}, \ldots\)? The frequency domain asks a completely different but equivalent question: how much of the series' variance is contributed by cyclical behaviour at each possible frequency?

6.2–6.3  The spectral distribution and density functions

Just as the ACF is the time-domain fingerprint of a process, the spectral density function \(f(\omega)\) is its frequency-domain fingerprint — and the two are, remarkably, just Fourier transforms of one another:

\[ f(\omega) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\gamma_k e^{-i\omega k} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\gamma_0 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\gamma_k\cos(\omega k)\right], \quad 0\le \omega \le \pi \]

Every autocovariance sequence has a spectral density and vice versa — no information is lost moving between the two domains; some structure is simply far easier to see in one than the other. A sharp peak in \(f(\omega)\) at some frequency \(\omega_0\) means the series behaves as though it contains a strong cyclical component with period \(2\pi/\omega_0\) — exactly the kind of thing that is easy to miss on a time plot but obvious in the spectrum.

\(f(\omega)=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{2\pi(1-2\phi\cos\omega+\phi^2)}\). Positive φ concentrates power at low frequencies (smooth, trend-like behaviour); negative φ concentrates it at high frequencies (rapid oscillation).

6.4–6.5  Continuous processes and selected spectra

White noise, having a flat ACF, has a perfectly flat spectral density — every frequency contributes equally, which is exactly the origin of the name "white" (by analogy with white light containing all frequencies equally). At the opposite extreme, a process dominated by trend concentrates almost all its power near \(\omega = 0\). These two textbook cases anchor the intuition for reading any spectrum: where the power sits tells you the character of the dependence.

Exercises

6.1
A sound engineer says "a flat spectrum means the signal is meaningless noise." A statistician says "a flat spectrum is exactly the definition of white noise, one of the most useful building blocks in the subject." Reconcile the apparent disagreement.
🔒 Reveal answer
They're not really disagreeing about the mathematics, only about what "meaningless" means. A flat spectrum says there is no linear, cyclical structure left to extract — every frequency contributes equally, so there's nothing more to model. That is exactly why white noise is the target residual in Chapter 4: it signals the model has extracted everything extractable. Whether the engineer calls that "meaningless" is a statement about what's useful to a listener, not about whether the mathematics is informative — to the statistician, "nothing more to explain" is itself a highly informative, desirable conclusion.
6.2
Without doing any calculation, sketch how the spectral density of an AR(1) process with φ close to +1 should look, and connect this shape to the fact that such a process is "close to" a (non-stationary) random walk.
🔒 Reveal answer
As φ → 1, the spectral density develops an increasingly sharp peak at ω = 0 and drops toward zero everywhere else — nearly all the variance is concentrated in the lowest ("slowest") frequency. This matches intuition: φ close to 1 means very strong persistence, so the series drifts slowly rather than oscillating, which is a low-frequency-dominated pattern. A true random walk (φ = 1 exactly) is non-stationary, so technically it has no well-defined spectral density in this framework at all — the AR(1) spectrum "blowing up" at ω = 0 as φ → 1 is the frequency-domain shadow of that same boundary case.
Chapter 7

Spectral Analysis

Chapter 6 defined the theoretical spectrum of a process. This chapter is about estimating it from one observed, finite series — which turns out to be surprisingly treacherous.

7.3  The periodogram

The natural first estimator is the periodogram, built directly from a discrete Fourier transform of the data:

\[ I(\omega_j) = \frac{1}{n}\left|\sum_{t=1}^{n} x_t\, e^{-i\omega_j t}\right|^2, \qquad \omega_j = \frac{2\pi j}{n} \]

Naively, you might expect the periodogram to converge nicely to the true spectrum \(f(\omega)\) as \(n\) grows — but it doesn't. \(I(\omega_j)\) is an inconsistent estimator: its variance does not shrink as more data is collected, so the raw periodogram stays jumpy and unreliable at any sample size.

7.4–7.6  Consistent estimation, confidence intervals, and comparing procedures

The fix is smoothing: average periodogram values over a small window of neighbouring frequencies (or equivalently, apply a lag window to the sample autocovariances before transforming). This trades a controlled amount of frequency resolution for a large reduction in variance — the same bias–variance trade-off that appears throughout statistics, here governed by the width of the smoothing window. Confidence intervals for the smoothed spectral estimate follow from the fact that (approximately) \(2\nu \hat f(\omega)/f(\omega)\) is chi-squared, with \(\nu\) (the equivalent degrees of freedom) increasing with window width.

Common pitfall — spectral leakage. A finite data record is implicitly like multiplying an infinite series by a rectangular window, which smears power from a true peak into neighbouring frequencies. Tapering the data at its ends (rather than cutting it off abruptly) reduces this leakage and is standard practice before computing a periodogram.

Exercises

7.1
"I collected ten times more data, so my periodogram should be ten times smoother." Correct this statement.
🔒 Reveal answer
More data does not, by itself, smooth the periodogram at all — it's inconsistent, meaning its variance at each frequency stays roughly constant regardless of n; what more data buys you is a finer grid of frequencies (\(\omega_j = 2\pi j/n\)), not lower variance at any one of them. Smoothness only improves by explicitly averaging over neighbouring frequencies (or an equivalent lag-window operation) — a deliberate modelling choice, not a free consequence of sample size.
7.2
Explain the bias–variance trade-off involved in choosing the width of a spectral smoothing window, using the specific language of resolution and equivalent degrees of freedom.
🔒 Reveal answer
A wide window averages over many neighbouring frequencies, which raises the equivalent degrees of freedom ν and shrinks the variance of the estimate — but it also blurs together frequencies that may have genuinely different power, biasing the estimate at any point toward the local average and losing the ability to resolve two closely-spaced peaks (poor resolution). A narrow window preserves resolution and has lower bias but leaves ν small, so the estimate stays noisy. There is no window width that is best for every purpose — it depends on whether the priority is detecting fine spectral structure or getting a stable overall picture.
Chapter 8

Bivariate Processes

Everything so far studied one series in isolation. Two series observed together raise a new question the ACF can't answer alone: does one lead, lag, or move in step with the other?

8.1  Cross-covariance and cross-correlation

The cross-correlation function (CCF) generalises the ACF to two series, and — critically — is not symmetric in the lag: \(\rho_{xy}(k)\) for positive \(k\) measures whether \(X\) leads \(Y\), while negative \(k\) measures whether \(Y\) leads \(X\).

\[ \rho_{xy}(k) = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X_t, Y_{t+k})}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X_t)\operatorname{Var}(Y_t)}} \]
X's peaks arrive k steps before Y's — a positive cross-correlation at lag k, read as "X leads Y by k periods."
8.2  The cross-spectrum

Just as the ACF has a frequency-domain counterpart, so does the CCF: the (complex-valued) cross-spectrum. Its normalised modulus, the coherence, plays the role of a frequency-specific correlation coefficient:

\[ \text{coherence}(\omega) = \frac{|f_{xy}(\omega)|^2}{f_x(\omega)f_y(\omega)} \in [0,1] \]

High coherence at a given frequency means the two series are strongly linearly related at that particular cycle length — even if their overall (unconditional) correlation is weak, because the relationship at other frequencies is washing it out.

✎ Worked example

Ice-cream sales and average daily temperature might show a strong annual cycle in both, high coherence near the "once-per-year" frequency — but very low overall correlation once you also account for a day-to-day promotional-pricing series that moves independently of temperature. Coherence isolates which cycle length is actually shared.

Exercises

8.1
An analyst finds \(\rho_{xy}(3) = 0.6\) and concludes "X causes Y with a 3-period delay." What is wrong with this conclusion, and what alternative explanation would produce exactly the same cross-correlation pattern?
🔒 Reveal answer
Correlation, cross-lagged or not, never establishes causation on its own. A high \(\rho_{xy}(3)\) is equally consistent with a common third factor driving both series with different delays (e.g. a seasonal cause that affects X three periods before it affects Y), with no direct causal link between X and Y at all. Genuine causal identification needs either a controlled intervention, a structural/economic argument ruling out common causes, or techniques (like Granger causality tests, which only test predictive precedence, not true causation) applied with those limitations explicitly acknowledged.
8.2
Two series have an overall (lag-0) correlation close to zero, yet a spectral analysis shows coherence close to 1 at the seasonal frequency. Is this a contradiction? Explain what's happening.
🔒 Reveal answer
No contradiction — overall correlation is a single number averaging the relationship across every frequency, while coherence is frequency-specific. The two series can move in near-lockstep at the seasonal cycle (high coherence there) while moving essentially independently at every other frequency; when averaged together, the strong seasonal relationship gets diluted by the lack of relationship elsewhere, producing an unremarkable overall correlation. This is exactly the kind of structure a single correlation coefficient is blind to and a coherence plot reveals immediately.
Chapter 9

Linear Systems

A linear system is a black box that turns an input series into an output series by a fixed, linear rule — a general framework that includes moving averages, differencing, and physical systems (a thermostat, a suspension bridge) as special cases.

A linear system reshapes the input's frequency content according to the transfer function H(ω), then noise is added.
9.2  Linear systems in the time domain

In the time domain, the output is a weighted sum of current and past inputs, \(Y_t = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} h_j X_{t-j} + N_t\), where \(\{h_j\}\) is the system's impulse response — literally, what the output looks like if the input were a single unit spike at time 0. Everything about a linear, time-invariant system is encoded in this one sequence.

9.3–9.4  The frequency domain, and identifying a system

Taking the Fourier transform of the impulse response gives the transfer function \(H(\omega)\) — a complex number, at each frequency, whose modulus tells you how much the system amplifies or attenuates that frequency, and whose phase tells you how much it delays it. Given input and output data, \(H(\omega)\) can be estimated from the ratio of the cross-spectrum to the input's own spectrum, \(\hat H(\omega) = \hat f_{xy}(\omega)/\hat f_{x}(\omega)\) — identifying the system's behaviour without ever writing down its internal mechanism explicitly.

Exercises

9.1
A 12-month centred moving average (Chapter 2) can be described as a linear system with a specific impulse response. What is \(h_j\) for this system, and what does its transfer function do to the annual seasonal frequency by design?
🔒 Reveal answer
The impulse response is the moving-average weights themselves: \(h_j = 1/12\) for \(j=-5,\ldots,5\), \(h_{\pm6}=1/24\), and 0 elsewhere (a finite, symmetric window). By design, its transfer function has a near-zero response exactly at the annual seasonal frequency (and its harmonics) — that's precisely why a 12-point average removes the yearly seasonal cycle from monthly data while passing the slower trend frequencies through largely unchanged. The whole "trend extraction" operation from Chapter 2 is just a specific low-pass linear filter.
9.2
Explain why the phase of a transfer function matters for forecasting even when its modulus (amplitude response) is identical to another system's.
🔒 Reveal answer
The modulus tells you how much a frequency component's amplitude is scaled, but the phase tells you how much it is shifted in time — two systems with identical amplitude responses but different phase responses will produce outputs that look similarly "shaped" in the frequency domain but arrive at different times relative to the input. For forecasting and control, that timing (lead/lag) information is often the entire point — knowing a shock's effect is delayed by two periods rather than arriving immediately changes exactly when you'd expect to see, and act on, its consequences.
Chapter 10

State-Space Models and the Kalman Filter

A different, often more flexible way to represent a time series: instead of writing one equation directly for the observed data, posit an unobserved (latent) state that evolves over time, and a separate equation for how the state is imperfectly observed.

10.1  State-space models
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{State (transition):} \quad & \alpha_t = T_t\,\alpha_{t-1} + R_t\,\eta_t \\ \text{Observation:} \quad & x_t = Z_t\,\alpha_t + \varepsilon_t \end{aligned} \]

This looks abstract, but it's extremely general: ARMA models, models with trend and seasonal components estimated simultaneously, and models with missing or irregularly-spaced data can all be written in this form. The "state" \(\alpha_t\) is whatever you believe is truly driving the series (e.g. its underlying level and trend) — you never observe it directly, only \(x_t\), a noisy measurement of it.

The Kalman filter loop: predict the state forward one step, then update that prediction once the next observation arrives — repeated for every new data point.
10.2  The Kalman filter

The Kalman filter is the algorithm that runs this predict/update cycle optimally (in the minimum mean square error sense, for linear-Gaussian models): it keeps a running estimate of the state and its uncertainty, and each new observation is blended with the prediction in proportion to their relative reliabilities — trust the new data more when the prediction is very uncertain, trust the existing estimate more when the new observation is noisy. This makes it naturally suited to real-time, online forecasting and to handling missing observations gracefully (simply skip the update step and use the predicted state as-is).

✎ Worked example

A GPS receiver's raw position fix is noisy from one instant to the next, but the underlying trajectory (a car driving down a road) evolves smoothly. A Kalman filter treats "true position and velocity" as the hidden state and each GPS ping as a noisy observation of it — blending the physically-plausible predicted trajectory with each new noisy fix produces a far smoother, more accurate track than trusting any single GPS reading alone.

Exercises

10.1
A sensor occasionally drops out entirely for several consecutive time points. Explain, structurally, why a state-space/Kalman-filter formulation handles this far more gracefully than a standard ARIMA model fitted by the Box–Jenkins method.
🔒 Reveal answer
In the state-space formulation, missing data simply means the update step is skipped for that time point — the predict step still runs, propagating the state (and its growing uncertainty) forward using the model dynamics alone, and the filter resumes normally the moment an observation reappears. Standard Box–Jenkins ARIMA fitting, by contrast, is built around a complete, evenly-spaced series; gaps have to be patched by imputation or otherwise worked around before the standard estimation machinery even applies, which is a structural retrofit rather than something the method handles natively.
10.2
Why does the Kalman filter weight a new observation more heavily precisely when its current state estimate is highly uncertain?
🔒 Reveal answer
The update step is a precision-weighted average of the predicted state and the new observation, where "precision" is the inverse of variance — so whichever source is currently more reliable (lower variance) naturally dominates the blend. When the predicted state is highly uncertain (e.g. after several skipped observations), its precision is low, so the new observation, however noisy in absolute terms, still carries more relative information and is weighted more heavily — this is simply optimal information-combining, not a special rule bolted onto the algorithm.
Chapter 11

Non-Linear Models

Every model so far has been linear: the current value is a linear combination of past values and past shocks. Many real phenomena — financial volatility, ecological populations, physiological signals — need more.

11.1–11.2  Why non-linear, and some models with non-linear structure

A linear model's ACF/PACF fully describes it; non-linear dependence can be completely invisible to the ACF while still being real and exploitable. A classic example is the threshold autoregressive (TAR) model, where the AR coefficients themselves switch depending on whether a past value crosses a threshold — letting a series behave differently in "regimes" (e.g. an economy's dynamics differing in recession vs. expansion) — something no single linear AR model can represent.

11.3  Models for changing variance

Financial returns are the textbook motivating case: they're often nearly uncorrelated (passing a Ljung–Box test easily) yet exhibit volatility clustering — large moves of either sign tend to be followed by more large moves. The ARCH model captures this by letting the conditional variance, not the mean, depend on the past:

\[ \varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t, \qquad \sigma_t^2 = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1\varepsilon_{t-1}^2, \qquad z_t \sim \text{i.i.d.}(0,1) \]

A large \(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2\) inflates \(\sigma_t^2\), making a large \(\varepsilon_t\) more likely too — clustering, without any autocorrelation in \(\varepsilon_t\) itself. GARCH generalises this by also feeding back \(\sigma_{t-1}^2\) itself, giving longer volatility memory with fewer parameters — exactly analogous to how ARMA improves on pure AR or MA.

Remember Exercise 4.1: this is precisely the structure that can lurk in "white noise" residuals that pass Ljung–Box — a linear model can be correctly specified for the mean and still miss all of this.
11.4  Neural networks

A feedforward neural network applied to lagged values (a "nonlinear AR") can, in principle, approximate essentially any smooth nonlinear relationship between past and present, at the cost of far weaker interpretability and a real risk of overfitting given typically modest time-series sample sizes — the same bias–variance story as everywhere else in the book, just with a much more flexible model class on the "low bias, high variance" end.

11.5  Chaos

Perhaps the most conceptually striking topic in the book: a completely deterministic nonlinear system can generate output that looks, statistically, indistinguishable from randomness. The canonical illustration is the logistic map:

\[ x_{n+1} = r\,x_n(1-x_n) \]

For small \(r\) this settles to a fixed point; as \(r\) increases it begins oscillating between 2, then 4, then 8 values (period-doubling); beyond \(r \approx 3.57\) it becomes chaotic — aperiodic, and exquisitely sensitive to the starting value, even though the rule generating it is a single, simple, fully deterministic equation with no randomness anywhere in it.

Exercises

11.1
A residual series from a well-fitted ARMA model passes the Ljung–Box test comfortably, yet its squared residuals show a strong, significant ACF. Name the phenomenon and the natural next modelling step.
🔒 Reveal answer
This is the signature of ARCH-type conditional heteroscedasticity — uncorrelated residuals whose magnitudes are autocorrelated (volatility clustering). Applying the Ljung–Box test to the squared (or absolute) residuals directly, rather than the raw residuals, is the standard diagnostic for exactly this; the natural next step is to fit an ARCH or GARCH model to the conditional variance on top of the existing mean model, rather than concluding (wrongly) from the first test alone that the model is fully adequate.
11.2
Two analysts look at the same chaotic logistic-map output. One says "this is clearly a random process — no deterministic pattern could look this irregular." The other says "this is fully deterministic — given x₀ and r exactly, the entire future is determined." Both are working from the same numbers. What does this tell you about trying to distinguish "truly stochastic" from "deterministic but chaotic" using only statistical tests on observed data?
🔒 Reveal answer
Standard statistical tests (ACF, Ljung–Box, spectral flatness) are built to detect linear dependence, and a chaotic deterministic series can pass all of them and look statistically identical to genuine randomness, because the nonlinear dependence it contains doesn't show up in linear diagnostics. The two analysts are both technically right about their own claim (it is deterministic; it does look statistically like noise) — the lesson is that "looks random by standard tests" and "is generated by a random mechanism" are not the same statement, and distinguishing them typically requires specialised nonlinear diagnostics (e.g. testing sensitivity to initial conditions), not the tools built in earlier chapters.
Chapter 12

Multivariate Time-Series Modelling

Chapter 8 looked at two series descriptively. Here, several series are modelled jointly, letting each one's past help explain and forecast all of them simultaneously.

A VAR model: each series depends on its own past (self-loops) and the other series' past (cross-arrows) — bidirectional feedback that single-equation models can't represent.
12.2–12.4  Single equation, VAR, and VARMA models

A single-equation model (e.g. regression with an ARMA error term) treats one series as the response and others as predictors — asymmetric, and only appropriate when the direction of influence is genuinely one-way. A vector autoregression, VAR(p), treats every series symmetrically: each one is regressed on lagged values of all the series, including itself.

\[ \mathbf{X}_t = \mathbf{A}_1\mathbf{X}_{t-1} + \cdots + \mathbf{A}_p\mathbf{X}_{t-p} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t \]

where \(\mathbf{X}_t\) is now a vector and each \(\mathbf{A}_i\) a matrix — off-diagonal entries capture exactly the cross-series, cross-lag feedback the diagram above illustrates. VARMA adds vector moving-average terms, at the cost of harder identification, which is why VAR alone remains the default workhorse for most applied multivariate work.

12.6  Co-integration

Two individually non-stationary (\(I(1)\)) series are cointegrated if some linear combination of them is stationary — meaning they share a common stochastic trend and never drift arbitrarily far apart, even though each one individually wanders without bound. This is the formal statistical language behind ideas like "prices of the same good in two connected markets track each other long-run" — modelling each series' differences separately would throw away exactly this long-run equilibrium relationship, which is why cointegrated series are instead modelled with a dedicated error-correction model.

Exercises

12.1
A researcher fits two separate univariate ARIMA models to two series that, in reality, feed back into one another (each one's past genuinely helps predict the other). Explain concretely what forecasting information this approach discards.
🔒 Reveal answer
Separate univariate models discard all cross-series predictive information — each model's forecast for its own series uses only that series' own past, completely ignoring the fact that the other series' recent movements might already contain an early signal about what's coming next. A VAR model captures exactly this by construction (the off-diagonal terms in each matrix \(\mathbf A_i\)), typically producing materially better forecasts whenever genuine bidirectional feedback exists — the whole point of modelling jointly rather than separately.
12.2
Two stock prices are each individually well-described as random walks (non-stationary), yet their difference (the "spread") appears to be stationary, hovering around a fixed level. What term describes this relationship, and why would naively differencing both series separately before modelling throw away useful information?
🔒 Reveal answer
This is cointegration — the two I(1) series share a common stochastic trend, and the stationary spread is the cointegrating relationship. Differencing both series separately would model only their period-to-period changes, which by construction discards the long-run level relationship between them (the fact that the spread itself is mean-reverting) — exactly the information that would let you predict, say, that an unusually wide spread is likely to narrow again. An error-correction model is built specifically to retain and exploit that mean-reverting relationship, which plain differencing throws away.
Chapter 13

Some Other Topics

A closing survey of practical concerns that don't fit neatly into earlier chapters but matter enormously in real applied work.

13.1–13.2  Model identification tools, and non-stationary series

Beyond the ACF/PACF, formal unit-root tests (e.g. the augmented Dickey–Fuller test) check statistically whether a series needs differencing rather than relying on visual judgement alone — important because over- or under-differencing both distort the subsequent model. Non-stationarity itself comes in different flavours worth telling apart: a stochastic trend (needs differencing) versus a deterministic trend (better modelled by regressing on time directly) look similar on a time plot but require different remedies.

13.3  The effect of model uncertainty

A subtle but important point: once a model's order is chosen by looking at the same data used to fit it, the usual formulas for forecast standard errors are slightly too optimistic — they don't account for the extra uncertainty introduced by the model-selection step itself. In practice this is usually a second-order concern, but it's a reminder that "the model" is itself an estimated quantity, not a known fact handed down before the data was seen.

13.4  Control theory

When a series is a process you can actively intervene in (Chapter 1's fourth objective), the natural next question becomes "what input should I apply now to keep the output on target?" This connects time series directly to control theory and feedback control systems — the same predict/adjust logic as the Kalman filter (Chapter 10), but now used to steer the process rather than merely track it.

Exercises

13.1
Two series both show a rising time plot. One is best modelled by regressing on calendar time directly (deterministic trend); the other needs differencing (stochastic trend). Describe one diagnostic difference you could look for in the data to tell them apart, beyond just "the plot goes up."
🔒 Reveal answer
A useful diagnostic is how the series' variance around its trend behaves as the horizon grows, or equivalently a formal unit-root test: a series with a genuine stochastic trend (like a random walk with drift) has a variance around any fitted deterministic line that grows without bound as more data is added, and shocks to it are permanent (they shift the whole future path). A series with a purely deterministic trend has stationary deviations around that trend line — shocks are transient and the series reverts back toward the fitted trend. An augmented Dickey–Fuller test formalises exactly this distinction rather than relying on eyeballing the time plot.

Mathematical Toolbox

Four compact reference appendices supply the mathematical machinery leaned on throughout — condensed here as quick-reference cards rather than full chapters.

A · Fourier, Laplace & Z transforms

Three related ways of re-expressing a signal in terms of frequency content. The Fourier transform underlies the spectral density (Ch. 6–7); the Z-transform is its discrete-time cousin, central to expressing AR/MA models compactly via the backshift operator \(B\), where \(BX_t = X_{t-1}\).

B · The Dirac delta function

An idealised "infinitely tall, infinitely narrow spike" with unit area, \(\int \delta(t)\,dt = 1\). It's the continuous-time idealisation of a single impulse — the natural input for probing a linear system's impulse response (Ch. 9).

C · Covariance

A refresher on \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]\) and its algebra — bilinearity, the relationship to correlation, and the variance-of-a-sum identity \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)+2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)\) — the identity that makes autocovariance-based reasoning throughout the book work.

Full, "warts and all" walkthroughs of real analyses end-to-end — deliberately including the messy judgement calls (which transformation? how many differences? which of two similar-AIC models?) that a chapter presenting only clean theory tends to hide.
