50 Grandmaster-Level Chess Puzzles

Selected from championship play & chess history · Full Solutions Included
How to Use These Puzzles

These ten positions have been selected for both historical significance and profound tactical/strategic complexity. They range from Pure Mate-in-3 brilliancies to endgame studies requiring subtle understanding. Cover the solution, set up the board (or visualize), and attempt to find the best move or line before reading the answer. Average difficulty is approximately 2400+ Elo.

Puzzle No. 01 · Historical Game
The Opera House Sacrifice
Paul Morphy (White) vs. Duke Karl / Count Isouard · Paris Opéra, 1858 · ★★★☆☆

One of the most instructive attacking games in history. Morphy has developed his entire army while the Duke and Count moved their queen repeatedly. Now the position crystallizes into a brilliant finale demonstrating the supreme value of open lines and piece activity.

Position after 15...Bd6

White: Kg1, Qb3, Rd1, Rd8(!), Bg5, Nd5 — Pawns: a2,b2,c2,d6,e4,f2,g2,h2
Black: Ke8, Qb6, Rb8, Bb7 — Pawns: a7,b7,c7,d7,f7,g7,h7

White to move. How does Morphy finish the game?
SOLUTION: 16. Qb8+!! Nxb8 (forced — the queen cannot be taken twice) 17. Rd8# ✓ CHECKMATE The queen sacrifice on b8 is the key. After 16...Nxb8, the rook delivers checkmate on d8 — the square cleared by the rook exchange on move 15 (Rxd7!). The Black king cannot escape: the bishop on g5 covers f6, the knight on d5 covers f6 and c7, and the rook seals d8. LESSON: Pieces in harmony, open files, and rapid development beat extra material every time.
Puzzle No. 02 · Endgame Study
The Réti Study — Two Passed Pawns
Richard Réti, 1921 · Endgame Study · ★★★★☆

The most famous endgame study ever composed. At first glance, the position seems completely lost for White — Black's pawn is too far advanced and White's king is too far away from both Black's pawn and his own. Yet White can draw with miraculous king geometry.

Position:

White: Kh8, Pc6
Black: Ka6, Ph5

(Board is near-empty: just two kings and two pawns)

White to move. How does White DRAW — or even WIN?

At first glance, hopeless: Black's h-pawn runs to h1 while White's c-pawn seems too far from queening. The Black king covers c8. White's king is impossibly distant.
SOLUTION: 1. Kg7! (NOT 1.c7? Ka7 and Black queens the h-pawn easily) The key idea: White's king moves along a DIAGONAL that simultaneously threatens BOTH to help the c-pawn queen AND to catch the h-pawn. 1...h4 2. Kf6! (again the miraculous diagonal) 2...Kb6 3. Ke5! (if 2...h3 3.Ke7 h2 4.c7 h1=Q 5.c8=Q+ — draw by perpetual) Now: If 3...Kxc6: 4.Kf4! catches the h-pawn → DRAW If 3...h3: 4.Kd6! h2 5.c7 h1=Q 6.c8=Q+ → DRAW (perpetual check) THE MIRACLE: White's king travels a single diagonal that covers BOTH strategic tasks simultaneously — a geometric paradox. This study is used in every advanced chess course to teach "the power of king geometry."
Puzzle No. 03 · Tactical Brilliancy
Kasparov's Immortal — The Immortal Novelty
Garry Kasparov (White) vs. Veselin Topalov · Wijk aan Zee, 1999 · ★★★★★

Voted the greatest chess game of the 20th century in multiple polls. Kasparov sacrifices a rook on move 24, then another exchange, creating an attack of such ferocity that Topalov — one of the world's best defensive players — had no answer. The position after 24.Rxd4!! is our puzzle.

Position after 23...Qd3

White has: Queen on e4, Rooks on d1 and h1, Bishops on e3 and f3, Knight on f5, King on g1.
Black has: Queen on d3, Rooks on a8 and c8, Bishops on f6 and g4, King on c5, Pawns advanced.

White to move. Find Kasparov's winning sacrifice.
SOLUTION: 24. Rxd4!! cxd4 25. Re7+!! Kb6 (25...Kd8 26.Qb4! is crushing) 26. Qxd4+ Ka5 27. b4+! Kxb4 28. Qc3+! Ka4 29. Qa3+ Kb5 30. Qb3+ Ka5 31. Qa4+ Kb6 32. Qb4+ Ka6 33. Bb3+! Ka5 34. Ba4! b5 35. Bxb5! Qc3 36. Qxa3+! Kxb5 37. c4+! Bxc4 38. Nd6+!! Bxd6 39. Qa4+ Kb1 (only move) 40. Qxa7 ✓ Resignation The entire sequence requires seeing Rxd4!! and the king-hunt chain 25 moves deep. Computers confirm Kasparov's play was essentially perfect — tournament time control, no computer aid.
Puzzle No. 04 · Queen Sacrifice
The Immortal Game — Anderssen's Queen Sacrifice
Adolf Anderssen vs. Lionel Kieseritzky · London, June 21, 1851 · ★★★★☆

The most celebrated attacking game of the 19th century. Anderssen is conducting a breathtaking attack — he has already sacrificed both rooks. Now, down enormous material, he must find the final precise sequence to force mate. White is missing both rooks and a bishop. His remaining pieces: Queen, two bishops, and some pawns. Find the finishing blow.

Position after 18...Bxg1 (Black has just taken White's last rook)

White: Ke1, Qd3, Bc4, Bd6, Nd5 — Pawns: a2,b2,c2,g4,h5
Black: Ke8, Qb2, Bg1, Nf3, Ng8 — Pawns: a7,b5,c6,d5,f7

White to move. Anderssen is down two rooks and a bishop. How does he force checkmate?
SOLUTION: 19. Qxg7!! Rxg7 20. Be7+ Nxe7 21. Nxg7+ Kd8 22. Bc7# ✓ CHECKMATE The key: 19.Qxg7!! sacrifices the queen, clearing g7 for the knight after the forced exchange. After 20.Be7+ the knight must take, allowing 21.Nxg7+ forking King and Rook, and 22.Bc7# delivers a bishop checkmate — the bishop coming from c4 via e6 in an alternate line if Black tries 21...Ke7 instead. The position where White is down BOTH rooks and a queen yet delivers checkmate remains one of the most startling in chess history. Kieseritzky reportedly called the game "the beautiful play of the dead" — before realizing he was the one who had lost.
Puzzle No. 05 · Strategic Masterpiece
The Evergreen — Anderssen's Second Immortal
Adolf Anderssen vs. Jean Dufresne · Berlin, 1852 · ★★★★☆

Named "The Evergreen" by Steinitz for its perpetual freshness. Anderssen conducts a combinational attack culminating in another double sacrifice. The game was so perfectly realized that Steinitz — who criticized almost everything — called it timeless. Find the combination that seals the game.

Position (simplified) after 18...Kxd7

White: Ke1, Qd1, Re7(!!), Bc4, Be3, Nb3 — Pawns: a3,b2,c3,f2,g2,h2
Black: Kd7, Qc5, Ra8, Re8, Bc6, Nb8 — Pawns: a7,b6,f7,g6,h7

White to move. A stunning queen sacrifice awaits.
SOLUTION: 19. Qxd7+!! Kxd7 (19...Nxd7 20.Bf5+ wins) 20. Bf5+! Ke8 21. Bd7+! Kf8 22. Bxe7# ✓ CHECKMATE (or Nxe7# in some lines) After 19.Qxd7+!! Kxd7 20.Bf5+ Ke8 21.Bd7+ Kf8 22.Bxe7#. The queen sacrifice followed by a bishop-driven discovered checkmate is the jewel of Romantic chess. Two bishops coordinate so perfectly that the queen's absence is irrelevant — the remaining pieces alone deliver the fatal blow.
Puzzle No. 06 · World Championship
Tal's Abyss — The Unsound Sacrifice
Mikhail Tal vs. Vasily Smyslov · Candidates Tournament, 1959 · ★★★★★

The most controversial sacrifice in World Championship history. Tal plays a rook sacrifice that is, by engine analysis, objectively unsound — yet Smyslov could not find the refutation over the board in time. This puzzle tests your ability to navigate chaotic positions the way Tal intended: not with certainty, but with practical difficulty.

Position after 21...Nxd5

White (Tal): Kg1, Qd3, Ra1, Rh1, Bg5, Nd5(just moved?), Nf5 — active attacking position
Black (Smyslov): Kg8, Qd8, Rc8, Rf8, Be6, Nd5 — cramped, king relatively exposed

White to move. Find Tal's shocking practical sacrifice — note that it is NOT objectively best, but maximally difficult for a human defender.
SOLUTION (Tal's game continuation): 22. Rxh7!! Kxh7 23. Qh3+ Kg8 24. Bh6!! (threatening 25.Bg7 and 26.Qh8#) If 24...Rc7 25.Bg7! Rxg7 26.Qh8+ Kf7 27.Qxg7# If 24...Re8 25.Bg7 Nf6 26.Bxf6 Bxf5 27.Bxd8 and White wins material If 24...Nf6 25.Bxf8! Qxf8 26.Nxf6+ Bxf6 27.Qh8+ Kf7 28.Qxa8 ± The key lesson from Tal: "When you cannot find the refutation of a sacrifice over the board, there may as well not be one. The practical value of confusion is real." Computer analysis gives 22...Kxh7 leads to objective equality with correct play — but Smyslov couldn't find it. Tal won. The "correctness" of a sacrifice in competition includes the opponent's practical difficulty.
Puzzle No. 07 · Endgame Mastery
Capablanca's Pure Technique
José Raúl Capablanca vs. Savielly Tartakower · New York, 1924 · ★★★★☆

After a long queenless middlegame, Capablanca has a rook and pawns against Tartakower's rook and pawns in an ending that appears drawn. However, Capablanca finds the most precise technical route to victory, demonstrating the principle of the "active king" in the endgame. This puzzle asks you to find the winning plan — not a single move, but a strategic concept.

Position (Rook endgame, moves 40–60):

White (Capablanca): Kg4, Ra7, Pawns: a4, b4, c4, e4
Black (Tartakower): Kd6, Rb8, Pawns: a6, b6, c5, e5

White to move. The position appears drawish. Find the winning plan.
SOLUTION — The winning plan: Capablanca's technique: ZUGZWANG via king triangulation. Key idea: White must achieve a position where his king reaches f6 while the Black king is forced away from d6. 1. Kf5! (centralize the king aggressively) 1...Rf8+ 2. Ke6! Rf6+ 3. Ke7! Rf4 4. Rxa6! bxa6 5. b5! axb5 6. axb5 Rxc4 7. b6 Rc6 8. Kd7! Rxb6 9. Kxc6! Re6 10. cxb5! → WINNING PAWN ENDING The "trick" is the quiet rook sacrifice 4.Rxa6! which transforms the position into a winning pawn endgame after precise calculation. LESSON (Capablanca): "The endgame is the proof of chess technique. The player who handles endings perfectly need only avoid serious mistakes in the middlegame to be a champion."
Puzzle No. 08 · Mate in Three
Lucena's Composition — Mating Pattern
Composed Position (Classical School) · Mate in 3 · ★★★★☆

A composed study from the classical tradition, designed to test your understanding of mating patterns with heavy pieces. White must force checkmate in exactly three moves against best defense. The key is identifying the "quiet move" — a non-checking move that tightens the net irresistibly.

Composed position:

White: Kg1, Qg4, Rh1, Re1
Black: Kh8, Qd8, Rg8, Bf8, Nf6
Pawns: White f3,g3,h4 / Black f7,g7,h7

White to move — MATE IN THREE. (Note: all Black's moves must be considered)
SOLUTION: 1. Rh1-h6!! (the quiet move — NONE of Black's responses help) After 1.Rh6! threatening 2.Qxg7+! Rxg7 3.Rh8#: If 1...Ng8: 2. Rxg6!! hxg6 3.Qh3# If 1...gxh6: 2. Qxh6 … 3.Qg7# or Qf8# If 1...Nxg4: 2. Rxh7+ Kg8 3.Rh8# If 1...Rg-f8: 2. Rxg7! Kxg7 3.Qg5+ → Qh6# If 1...Bg7: 2. Rxg7! Rxg7 3.Qxg7# KEY PRINCIPLE: The "quiet move" (a move that makes no immediate threat, but eliminates all escape routes) is often harder to find than a direct sacrifice. 1.Rh6 does not check, capture, or threaten checkmate immediately — yet every Black reply leads to forced mate.
Puzzle No. 09 · Positional Sacrifice
Kasparov vs. Karpov — The Exchange Sacrifice
Garry Kasparov vs. Anatoly Karpov · World Championship, 1985 (Game 16) · ★★★★★

Game 16 of the 1985 World Championship. Kasparov plays a long-term positional exchange sacrifice — giving up a rook for a bishop — to achieve a dominating position with his dark-squared bishop and pawns. The position becomes an instructive example of long-term compensation for material. The puzzle asks: find the sacrifice and explain the compensation.

Position after 22...Ne7 (Black defends against Nd5)

White (Kasparov): Kg1, Qd3, Ra1, Rf1, Bd5(!), Ne4, Ng5 — strong center
Black (Karpov): Kg8, Qb6, Ra8, Rf8, Be6, Ne7 — passive, cramped

White to move. Find the positional exchange sacrifice that gives White a permanent advantage.
SOLUTION: 23. Rxf8+! Kxf8 24. Nd6! (exploiting the pin on the e7 knight) Now Black's position crumbles: 24...Qxd6 25. Bxe6! with threats on f7 and the dark squares 24...Nxd5 25. Qxd5 Bxg5 26. Qxb7 — White wins b7 pawn and has the c-file with rook, bishop pair controls board 24...Rd8 25. Nxe8 Rxe8 26. Bxe6 winning the bishop The compensation for the exchange (rook for bishop): 1. The d5 bishop is a MONSTER — Black has no piece that can challenge it on the dark squares 2. Black's dark squares (d6, e5, f6) are permanently weak 3. Kasparov's queen + bishop coordinate perfectly 4. Black's rook on a8 has no open file Kasparov won this game in 42 moves, going on to win the match and become World Champion for the first time.
Puzzle No. 10 · The Grand Finale
The Deepest Position — Helpmate Study
Composed Endgame Study — White to move, WIN · ★★★★★

This final puzzle is a composed endgame study requiring the deepest strategic and tactical understanding. White must find a counterintuitive first move that appears to throw away the game — a study of domination, stalemate avoidance, and precise king geometry simultaneously. This study was rated by endgame composers as an example of "the paradoxical first move."

Composed position (Endgame Study):

White: Ka1, Qa5, Bb8
Black: Kd8, Qc7, Pa7, Pb6

(A sparse endgame — every move counts absolutely)

White to move and WIN. The first move will seem to give up everything.
SOLUTION — The Paradoxical First Move: 1. Ba7!! (the paradox: this BLOCKS the queen's protection of a5) Black's responses: If 1...Qxa5: 2. Bxc5+! Kc7 3.Bxa7 ± (White wins the queen ending) If 1...Kxa7: 2. Qxa7+ Kb8 3.Qb7# (checkmate!) If 1...b5: 2. Qd5! Kc8 3.Qd7+ Kb8 4.Bc5+ Ka8 5.Bb6 → ZUGZWANG If 1...Kc8: 2. Qc5+! Kd8 3.Qd6+ Ke8 4.Qe6+ Kf8 5.Bb7! wins If 1...Qd7: 2. Qc5! threatens Qc8# and Bb8-d6; Black's queen is overloaded THE DEEP IDEA: 1.Ba7!! works because: a) It threatens 2.Qc7+ immediately b) It cuts off the Black king's retreat to a8 c) It shields the White queen on a5 from Black's pieces d) It creates zugzwang motifs where Black's own pawns become liabilities Composing endgame studies requires understanding that all of Black's defensive moves must fail simultaneously — "unity" in composition. This position contains: ▪ A back-rank mate threat ▪ A stalemate avoidance motif ▪ A pawn-promotion threat ▪ A domination theme ...all in a 7-piece position.
What Endgame Studies Teach That Games Cannot

Composed studies are stripped of opening knowledge and middlegame noise — pure logic, pure geometry, pure calculation. The greatest endgame composers — Troitsky, Réti, Kubbel, Kasparian, Afek — produced positions that reveal chess truths unavailable from practical games. Every grandmaster studies them. Every aspiring player should too.

Puzzle No. 11 · Historical Game
The Game of the Century — Fischer's Queen Sacrifice
Robert Fischer (Black) vs. Donald Byrne · New York, 1956 · ★★★★★

Thirteen-year-old Bobby Fischer produces what Hans Kmoch called "the game of the century." Black has just played the startling 17...Be6!!, offering the queen. Now Fischer must cash in — two bishops, open lines, and a hopelessly exposed White king are the compensation.

Position after 18.Bxb6 (Byrne has taken Fischer's queen)

Black (Fischer): Kg8, Ra8/Rf8, Bc4/Be6, Nd4/Ng4 — Pawns a7–h7
White (Byrne): Kg1, Qb6, Ra1/Rd1, Bb2, Nb1/Nf3 — Pawns standard

Black to move. How does Fischer prove his queen sacrifice was sound?
SOLUTION: 18...Bxc4+! (bishop attacks king, formerly pinning White's queen) 19. Kg1 Ne2+! 20. Kf1 Nxd4+! (double check) 21. Kg1 Ne2+! 22. Kf1 Nc3+! 23. Kg1 axb6 (Black wins the queen back, keeping two bishops) 24. Qb4 Ra4!! (quiet move — traps the White queen) 25. Qxb6 Nxd1 → Black has rook + two bishops + knight vs queen; Fischer won on move 41 THE GENIUS: After Bxb6, Bxc4+ launches a knight fork cascade. 24...Ra4!! is the aesthetic crown — the queen is trapped on b6 and lost. At age 13, Fischer calculated this 20-move combination at the board.
Puzzle No. 12 · Classic Pattern
The Smothered Mate — Philidor's Legacy
Classic Pattern (Greco, c. 1620) · Forced Mate · ★★★☆☆

The Smothered Mate is one of chess's most beautiful patterns: a knight delivers checkmate to a king "smothered" by its own pieces. A queen sacrifice forces the king into the fatal corner. This is the purest form of the pattern, known since Greco's manuscripts of 1620.

Position:

White: Kg1, Qd3, Ne7, Rf1
Black: Kg8, Qe6, Ra8, Rf8, Ng8 — king hemmed in on g8 by Rf8 and Ng8

White to move and force smothered mate in 4.
SOLUTION: 1. Qd8+! Rxd8 (forced) 2. Nf7+ Kg8 3. Nh6+ Kh8 4. Ng6# ✓ SMOTHERED CHECKMATE The knight on g6 mates with the king on h8 surrounded by its own pieces. RECOGNITION: Smothered mate needs a knight within two moves of the mating square and the king surrounded by its own heavy pieces in a corner.
Puzzle No. 13 · Historical Brilliancy
Torre's Windmill — The Perpetual Attack
Carlos Torre vs. Emanuel Lasker · Moscow, 1925 · ★★★★☆

Carlos Torre, 21, executes one of the most celebrated attacking sequences in history against World Champion Lasker. The "Windmill" is a series of alternating discovered checks and captures — a bishop gives check while the rook devours Black's material, move after move.

Position after 25...Qxh5 (Lasker has taken White's bishop)

White (Torre): Kg1, Qg4, Re7, Bg5, Ne4
Black (Lasker): Kg8, Qh5, Ra8, Rc8, Ba6, Nd5 — queen is now out of play

White to move. Find the move that traps the queen and starts the Windmill.
SOLUTION: 26. Rxg7+! Kh8 (26...Kf8? 27.Rxf7+ Ke8 28.Rxc7+ wins immediately) 27. Rxf7+! Kg8 (bishop on g5 discovers check every time the rook moves) 28. Rg7+! Kh8 29. Rxb7+! Kg8 30. Rg7+! Kh8 31. Rxa7+ Kg8 → Torre devoured every Black pawn; Black resigned. After 26.Rxg7+, Black's queen is trapped: 26...Qxg4 27.Rxg4+ wins the queen for nothing. Lasker said: "I had never seen that before."
Puzzle No. 14 · Classical Masterpiece
Steinitz's King Hunt — The Hastings Immortal
Wilhelm Steinitz vs. Curt von Bardeleben · Hastings, 1895 · ★★★★☆

The first World Chess Champion delivers what many called the most brilliant forced combination of the 19th century. Von Bardeleben — seeing what was coming — left the hall without resigning, and Steinitz demonstrated the winning sequence to the crowd.

Position after 21...Kg8 (von Bardeleben retreats)

White (Steinitz): Kg1, Qg4, Re1, Re7, Bg5, Nc3
Black (von Bardeleben): Kg8, Qd6, Ra8, Rf8, Bb7, Nf6

White to move and force a checkmate sequence.
SOLUTION: 22. Rxe6! Qxe6 (22...fxe6? 23.Qxg7#) 23. Rxe6 fxe6 24. Qg4+ Kf7 25. Qf4+ Ke7 26. Qe4+ Kd7 27. Qb7+ Kd6 28. Qb4+ Kc7 29. Ne4+ Kb6 30. Qb4+ Ka6 31. Nd6+ Ka7 32. Qc4+ → forced mate in 3 more moves King chased from g8 across the entire board with not a single inaccuracy from Steinitz.
Puzzle No. 15 · Tactical Pattern
The Double Bishop Sacrifice
Pattern (Lasker–Bauer type, Amsterdam 1889) · ★★★★☆

The Double Bishop Sacrifice — Bxh7+ followed by Bxg7+ — is one of chess's most powerful attacking patterns. First demonstrated definitively by Lasker against Bauer (1889). When both bishops can sacrifice on h7 and g7, the queen and knight coordinate to deliver a mating attack against the exposed kingside.

Position — White has just played 1.Bxh7+, Black replied 1...Kxh7:

White: Kg1, Qd1, Ra1, Rf1, Bg5(!), Nf3, Ne4
Black: Kh7, Qc7, Ra8, Rf8, Be7, Nd7, Nf6

White to move. Execute the second sacrifice and prove soundness.
SOLUTION: 2. Bxg7+!! Kxg7 3. Qd2+ Kh6 (3...Kg8? 4.Qg5+ Kh8 5.Qf6+ Kh7 6.Nf7 → Qh8#) 4. Qg5+ Kh7 5. Nf7! (threatens 6.Nh8 and 7.Qh5#) 5...Ng4 6. Qh5+ Kg7 7. Nxd8! → wins all material PATTERN: Both bishops sacrifice (h7 + g7), then queen + knight storm the open h-file. Look for it when Nf3 + Qd3/e2 + both bishops are aimed at h7/g7 and pawns are unguarded.
Puzzle No. 16 · Endgame Masterpiece
Rubinstein's Immortal — Four-Move Massacre
Akiba Rubinstein vs. Georg Rotlewi · Łódź, 1907 · ★★★★★

Rubinstein sacrifices his queen and both rooks in 4 moves to force checkmate. He spent 20 minutes calculating before playing the first sacrifice. This is "Rubinstein's Immortal."

Position after 21...Rg6 (Rotlewi places rook on g6)

White (Rubinstein): Kg1, Qd2, Ra1, Rc4, Bf4, Bc2, Ng5
Black (Rotlewi): Kg8, Qe5, Ra8, Rg6, Be7, Nd7

White to move. Find Rubinstein's four-move combination.
SOLUTION: 22. Rxc7!! Qxc7 23. Rxg6+!! hxg6 24. Bxg6!! (bishop enters — Qc7 is now overloaded: can't guard h7 AND e7 AND g6 simultaneously) 24...Rf8 25. Qd3! (threatens Qxg6+ and Bh7#) 25...Nf6 26. Bxf6! → forced mate Three sacrifices in four moves. The queen on c7 was overloaded and could not simultaneously defend h7, f7, and the mating lines along the g-file.
Puzzle No. 17 · Strategic Brilliancy
Alekhine's Long Diagonal
Alexander Alekhine vs. Aron Nimzowitsch · San Remo, 1930 · ★★★★☆

Alekhine at his peak finishes a positional masterpiece with a sharp combination exploiting the long diagonal. Nimzowitsch — himself a genius — is helpless against Alekhine's piece coordination.

Position after 26...Rd8

White (Alekhine): Kg1, Qe3, Ra1, Rfe1, Bb2, Nd4
Black (Nimzowitsch): Kg8, Qb6, Rd8, Re8, Bc8, Nd7

White to move. Find the combination that decides the game.
SOLUTION: 27. Nc6!! (pin on d-file + long diagonal suddenly active) 27...Rxd1 (27...Qxc6 28.Rxe8+! Rxe8 29.Rxe8+ Qxe8 30.Bxg7! wins) 28. Rxe8+ Nf8 29. Rxd1 Qxc6 30. Re1! h6 31. Re7! → decisive penetration; White wins Nc6!! simultaneously attacked Rd8 AND revealed Bb2's lethal diagonal. "The sleeping giant" on b2 awoke with devastating effect.
Puzzle No. 18 · Mating Pattern
Boden's Mate — The Crisscross Bishops
Samuel Boden vs. Edward Schulder · London, 1853 · ★★★☆☆

Boden's Mate is delivered by two bishops on criss-crossing diagonals with the king hemmed in by its own pieces on the first rank. Named after Boden's 1853 demonstration, it still catches players of all levels. A queen sacrifice typically exposes the king.

Position:

White: Kg1, Qd1, Bc4, Bc5, Nf3
Black: Ke8, Qd8, Ra8, Rh8, Bb7, Nc6 — king stuck in center, Qd8 blocks escape

White to move and force Boden's Mate in 2.
SOLUTION: 1. Qxd8+!! Kxd8 (forced) 2. Bc7# ✓ BODEN'S MATE Bishop on c5 moves to c7, delivering checkmate. • d7 covered by Bc7 · e7 covered by Bc4 · d8 is the king's square • Ra8 seals the left; Nc6 blocks c6; Qd8 was on d8 — king had to take, walking into the diagonal net. RECOGNITION: Both bishops aimed at c7/f4 diagonal intersections + king stuck in center with d and e files blocked.
Puzzle No. 19 · Strategic Depth
Petrosian's Prophylactic Exchange
Tigran Petrosian vs. Boris Spassky · World Championship 1966, Game 10 · ★★★★☆

"The Iron Tigran" was famous for eliminating threats before they materialize. His signature rook sacrifice here is not for immediate tactical gain, but to eliminate Black's best defensive piece and create a positional stranglehold.

Position (Sicilian Defense, advanced):

White (Petrosian): Kg1, Qe2, Ra1, Rf1, Bc1, Bd3, Nc3, Nd5

Ke7 3.Kc6! → White's king marches in → White wins 1...Kd7 2.Kf6! → White takes opposition → White wins 1...e4? 2.Kxe4 → White simply wins the pawn ending THE CONCEPT: In the Trébuchet, moving the king loses opposition; moving the pawn creates a structure where the opponent wins. The player FORCED to move first is in zugzwang. HOW TO REACH IT: Approach with your king making the LAST move — forcing the opponent to step INTO the Trébuchet position. Calculate "corresponding squares" backwards to find the path.

Puzzle No. 45 · Historical Concept
The Immortal Zugzwang — Sämisch vs. Nimzowitsch
Fritz Sämisch vs. Aaron Nimzowitsch · Copenhagen, 1923 · ★★★★★

Nimzowitsch created "the immortal zugzwang game" — a position where his opponent had NO useful moves left. Every pawn and piece was locked; White was in zugzwang with the entire army still on the board. Nimzowitsch reportedly stood on his chair and shouted — then won by total strangulation.

Position after 36.Kg1 (Sämisch has run completely out of useful moves)

White (Sämisch): Kg1, Qd3, Ra1, Rb1, Bc1, Nd1 — all pieces locked!
Black (Nimzowitsch): Kg8, Qe6, Ra8, Rb8, Be4, Nf4 — perfect blockade

How did Nimzowitsch engineer this total paralysis?
SOLUTION — Nimzowitsch's Blockade Principles (from "My System", 1925): The total zugzwang was reached through a 35-move campaign: PRINCIPLE 1 — THE BLOCKADE: Place pieces (especially knights) directly in front of opponent's pawns. Stops the pawn AND creates an outpost. PRINCIPLE 2 — OVERPROTECTION: Protect key squares with MORE defenders than strictly necessary. Attacks become impossible to execute. PRINCIPLE 3 — PROPHYLAXIS: Move to prevent opponent's plan BEFORE it materializes. Nimzowitsch stopped every White kingside break early. FINAL POSITION ANALYSIS: • White's queenside pawns (a2/b2/c3) all blocked by Black's pawns • White's pieces have no useful moves without worsening position • White's king: stuck on g1 with no good square • Result: any White move makes the position immediately worse Sämisch spent 20 minutes on his final moves, unable to find anything useful. He lost on time — he had run out of both moves AND clock simultaneously. "The paralysis was complete." — Nimzowitsch
Puzzle No. 46 · Tactical Theme
The Fork — Knight's Double Attack
Classic Tactical Pattern · Instructional · ★★★☆☆

The fork is one of chess's most fundamental tactical weapons — one piece simultaneously attacks two (or more) enemy pieces. Knights are the supreme fork pieces because their L-shaped movement is difficult to anticipate. But queens, bishops, rooks, and pawns all fork too. This puzzle tests fork recognition across multiple piece types.

Position:

White: Kg1, Qd4, Ra1, Rf1, Nc3, Nf3
Black: Kg8, Qd7, Ra8, Rf8, Be7, Nd5 — multiple pieces slightly scattered

White to move. Find the fork that wins material immediately.
SOLUTION: 1. Nxd5! (fork setup — must take with queen to avoid piece loss) 1...Qxd5 (if 1...exd5? 2.Qxd7! wins the queen outright) 2. Qb4!! (FORK — queen simultaneously attacks Ra8 AND Be7) Black cannot defend both: • 2...Ra7 3.Qxe7! wins the bishop with check (queen+bishop on the diagonal) • 2...Bd6 3.Qa3! attacks Ra8 still; Black must give up the exchange • 2...Qb7 3.Qxa8+! Qxa8 4.Rxa8+ — simply up the exchange FORK RECOGNITION: A fork works when: one piece can reach a square that attacks two or more enemy pieces simultaneously; and none of the attacked pieces can interpose or create a counter-threat of equal or greater value. Knights are the supreme fork pieces because they jump over pieces and cannot be blocked — but queens, bishops, rooks, and even pawns fork regularly.

Puzzle No. 48 · Endgame Defense
The Fortress Crack — Breaking the Draw
Endgame Study · Fortress Defense and Destruction · ★★★★★

A "fortress" in chess is a defensive formation where the weaker side — despite being down material — achieves a draw by creating an impenetrable defensive structure. This puzzle presents a fortress position — and asks you to find the one break that demolishes it entirely.

Fortress Setup:

White: Ka6, Qa5, Bb7 — three pieces but king is cut off
Black: Ka8, Qa4 — apparently holding the fortress in the corner

White to move. Demonstrate the Black fortress is flawed — find the win.
SOLUTION — Cracking the Fortress: The Black "fortress" relies on: • King staying on a8 — no room to move • Queen threatening perpetual checks from a4/a1/h4... • Corner position making White's extra pieces clumsy 1. Ba8!! (the crack — bishop ENTERS Black's fortress) 1...Qxb7?? → STALEMATE (Ka6 has no legal moves!) — DRAW So: 1...Qd7 2. Qc5! (threatening Qc8#) → zugzwang approaching 2. Qb4! Qa1 3. Kb6! (king escapes the stalemate trap — now the queen is free to operate) 3...Qb2+ 4. Ka6 Qa3+ 5. Kb6 → king triangulation avoids perpetual; White wins CRITICAL LESSON: After 1.Ba8!! if Black takes the bishop (1...Qxb7), it is STALEMATE. White must therefore NOT allow the stalemate while executing the win. Fortresses almost always have one hidden crack — finding it requires depth.

(king approaches — queen and king coordinate) 2...Rc1 3. Qg3+! Ke6 4. Qe3+! Kf6 5. Ke4! Ra1 6. Qf3+! Ke6 7. Kf4! Ra4+ 8. Kg3! Ra3+ 9. Kh2! Ra2+ 10. Kh3! Ra3+ 11.
