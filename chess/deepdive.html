Famous Games, Theory & Records

The Immortal Game — Annotated

Adolf Anderssen vs. Lionel Kieseritzky · London 1851 · King's Gambit

The most celebrated attacking game of the 19th century. Anderssen sacrifices one knight, two rooks, and his queen to force checkmate with just two bishops and a knight. Named by Ernst Falkbeer, who published it in La Régence as "die unsterbliche Partie."

1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Bc4 Qh4+ 4.Kf1 b5 5.Bxb5 Nf6 6.Nf3 Qh6 7.d3 Nh5 8.Nh4 Qg5 9.Nf5 c6 10.g4 Nf6 11.Rg1! cxb5 12.h4! Qg6 13.h5 Qg5 14.Qf3! Ng8 15.Bxf4 Qf6 16.Nc3 Bc5 17.Nd5 Qxb2 18.Bd6! Bxg1 19.Qxg7!! Rxg7 20.Be7+ Nxe7 21.Nxg7+ Kd8 22.Bc7# 1-0
Key Annotations
  • 11.Rg1! — Voluntarily en prise; secures the g-file. Kieseritzky grabs it greedily.
  • 18.Bd6! — Quiet blockade removes the last defender of g7.
  • 19.Qxg7!! — Queen sacrifice. White is down both rooks + queen yet delivers mate in three. Extraordinary for 1851.

The Pearl of Reykjavik — Game 6, Fischer-Spassky 1972

Robert Fischer vs. Boris Spassky · World Championship Reykjavik · Queen's Gambit Declined, Tartakower

Fischer, an almost exclusive 1.e4 player, played 1.d4. The resulting positional masterpiece combined Capablanca-level strategic clarity with Fischer's meticulous preparation. When Fischer played his key pawn advance on move 25, Spassky stood up and applauded — then resigned on move 41.

1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6 3.Nf3 Nf6 4.Nc3 Be7 5.Bg5 0-0 6.e3 h6 7.Bh4 b6 8.cxd5 Nxd5 9.Bxe7 Qxe7 10.Nxd5 exd5 11.Rc1 Be6 12.Qa4! c5 13.Qa3! Rc8 14.Bb5! a6 15.dxc5 bxc5 16.0-0! Ra7 17.Be2 Nd7 18.Nd4! Qf8 19.Nxe6! fxe6 20.e4! d4 21.f4! Qe7 22.e5! Rcc7 23.Be2-f3 ... 25.b4!! [key advance] 1-0 (move 41)
The Standing Ovation

Boris Spassky, still at the board after playing his 41st move and seeing Fischer's reply, stood up and led the audience in applause for his opponent before shaking hands to resign. A gesture unique in World Championship history — and one of the most sportsmanlike moments in any sport.

Kasparov vs. Topalov 1999 — The Game of the Century

Garry Kasparov vs. Veselin Topalov · Wijk aan Zee 1999 · Pirc Defense

Voted the greatest chess game of the 20th century in multiple polls. Kasparov plays a rook sacrifice on move 24 (Rxd4!!) that initiates a king-hunt lasting 16 moves, pursued with machine-like accuracy yet under tournament conditions with no computer assistance. The final position after 44.Qa4+ Kb1 45.Qxa7 cannot be reached without seeing the entire cascade from move 24.

1.e4 d6 2.d4 Nf6 3.Nc3 g6 4.Be3 Bg7 5.Qd2 c6 6.f3 b5 7.Nge2 Nbd7 8.Bh6 Bxh6 9.Qxh6 Bb7 10.a3 e5 11.0-0-0 Qe7 12.Kb1 a6 13.Nc1 0-0-0 14.Nb3 exd4 15.Rxd4! c5 16.Rd1 Nb6 17.g3 Kb8 18.Na5 Ba8 19.Bh3 d5 20.Qf4+ Ka7 21.Rhe1 d4 22.Nd5 Nbxd5 23.exd5 Qd6 24.Rxd4!! cxd4 25.Re7+ Kb6 26.Qxd4+ Ka5 27.b4+! Kxb4 28.Qc3+ Ka4 29.Qa3+ Kb5 30.Qb3+ Ka5 31.Qa4+ Kb6 32.Qb4+ Ka6 33.Bb3+ Ka5 34.Ba4! b5 35.Bxb5! Qc3 36.Qxa3+ Kxb5 37.c4+ Bxc4 38.Nd6+ Bxd6 39.Qa4+ Kb1 40.Qxa7 1-0

The FIDE Rating System

Elo — How Chess Measures Mastery

The Elo rating system, designed by Hungarian-American physics professor Arpad Elo and adopted by FIDE in 1970, is the mathematical backbone of chess ranking — later adopted by sports, video games, and competitive fields worldwide. New Rating = Old Rating + K × (Actual Score − Expected Score), where K is a volatility factor and Expected Score is calculated from the rating difference between players.

The Mathematics of Chess — Numbers Beyond Imagination

Scale of Chess Complexity
  • Possible legal positions: ~1043 — the Shannon estimate
  • Possible games (Shannon Number): ~10120 — vastly exceeds atoms in the observable universe (~1080)
  • Tablebases: All 7-piece positions solved — ~549 trillion positions with perfect play known
  • Stockfish search depth: 30–40 half-moves (plies) in typical middlegame; millions of positions per second
  • Is chess solved? Almost certainly a theoretical draw with perfect play, but unprovable with current or foreseeable computation
  • After 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 — possible positions by move 10: approximately 69 trillion
  • Storage required for 8-piece tablebases: Approximately 100+ petabytes — beyond current practical storage
